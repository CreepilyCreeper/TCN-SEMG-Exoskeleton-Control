#### 2.1. Phase 1: Literature Review Summary

##### 2.1.1. The role of exoskeletons in construction

Work‑related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) are a leading occupational health burden in construction. Systematic syntheses report pooled prevalence estimates between 59-64% with clear regional heterogeneity (Asia ≈63%, America ≈39%, Africa ≈52%) [2]. Large cross‑sectional surveys corroborate these magnitudes locally (e.g., 57.9% in Guangdong Province) [15]. [15] and [21] identify the lower back and shoulders among the most affected regions, with [2] additionally reporting knees ≈23% across its included studies. This matches the exposure rate to straining actions such as repetitive lifting, awkward postures and prolonged overhead tasks [15], [21]. National surveillance and industry reports translate this burden into large economic costs and lost work‑time, amplifying the policy imperative to evaluate effective preventive controls in sectors with ageing and physically intensive workforces [1], [2].

Exoskeletons occupy a specific prevention niche as engineering controls intended to redistribute loads, support joints, or augment muscular torque during high‑risk tasks. Aggregate and device‑level evidence indicates consistent biomechanical benefits in controlled conditions: passive back supports commonly reduce lower‑back EMG by ~3-11% [21], knee‑assist devices can cut knee muscle activation by up to ~39% [1], and powered or semi‑active systems can shift joint moments in ways that plausibly reduce tissue loading [30]. Complementary outcomes reported in experimental deployments include productivity increases and fatigue mitigation; one 2025 experimental study observed task‑level productivity gains from ~10% up to 59.5% in high‑intensity tasks and concurrent reductions in sway metrics, although gains were task‑ and device‑dependent and amplified by short training/adaptation periods [25].

Authoritative syntheses (e.g., NIOSH briefings) and systematic reviews therefore frame exoskeletons as promising but conditional interventions: effectiveness depends on appropriate device‑task matching, correct fit and donning procedures, and organizational supports such as training, maintenance and monitoring [1], [19]. Two consistent caveats appear across reviews. First, transferability: most primary evidence remains short‑term and laboratory‑biased (such as ≈77-78% of studies in controlled settings in [19]), relying frequently on convenience samples rather than construction workers, which inflates internal validity but weakens external generalizability for procurement and OHS policy [19], [21]. Second, risk transfer and equity: devices can introduce new hazards (altered center‑of‑gravity, snagging, pressure injuries) and raise ethical concerns around coercion, unequal access, and sensitive data collection unless integrated into formal OHS and procurement frameworks with clear governance [5], [20], [16], [18]. Consequently, reviews recommend treating exoskeletons as elements of multi‑layered risk control hierarchies rather than stand‑alone fixes, embedding them in risk assessments, PPE compatibility checks and informed procurement processes [6], [8], [23].

##### 2.1.2. Physical design: progress and limitations

Design trajectories split broadly into soft exosuits (textile‑based assistance), modular rigid frames for high‑force tasks, and task‑specific attachments such as gloves, chairless seating, and arm/back supports [4], [13], [22]. Soft systems offer low profile, compliance, and wearer comfort; representative prototypes report hip assistance up to ~30% of biological joint torque during gait [17], and pneumatic knee actuators capable of generating laboratory torques (e.g., up to 324 Nm) competitive with therapeutic devices [4], [17], suggesting potential applicability for kneeling and stooping tasks in construction when re‑engineered for industrial robustness. Rigid, heavy‑duty systems can provide larger absolute assistance (e.g., lift assistance of ≈15 kg for specific exoskeleton types [13]) and are attractive for heavy lifting phases, but their bulk and inertia commonly impair maneuverability in constrained or overhead work [13].

Field‑oriented constraints recur across qualitative and quantitative studies. Misalignment between device and anatomical joints produces shear and focal pressure that undermines comfort and may create secondary injury risks; bulky frames reduce reach and mobility on scaffolds or in trenches; thermal burden and interference with PPE (hard hats, harnesses) limit continuous wear in hot, dusty environments [5], [20], [22], [26]. Economic barriers compound these ergonomic issues: product prices span roughly US\$5,000-\$100,000 [8], limiting SME procurement and encouraging shared‑use or rental models as stopgaps [19]. Mixed‑methods and Delphi research further prioritise equity concerns - non‑gender‑neutral sizing, don/doff injury vulnerability and weight were rated as high‑impact risks - demanding inclusive design and procurement policies to avoid widening disparities [16], [18].

Engineering guidance therefore emphasizes modular, lightweight architectures with adjustable coupling mechanisms to minimize misalignment; rapid don/doff systems compatible with standard PPE; thermal management through breathable textiles; and task‑specific attachments that avoid excessive bulk for low‑force jobs [17], [10], [23]. Advances in sensing and controller design also inform physical requirements: sensing‑to‑control syntheses recommend prioritizing fewer, robust sensors and fewer skin‑contact elements to improve wearability and reduce setup time [28], while task‑agnostic joint‑moment controllers demonstrate that onboard inference hardware and sensor placement choices materially affect device mass and attachment strategy - for example, task‑agnostic implementations have reported added device mass of 4.8 kg [31] and 7 kg [30] and require integrated IMUs, encoders, and pressure insoles for reliable moment estimation, with direct implications for coupling design and thermal management. Practical translation therefore requires standardized, task‑oriented evaluation protocols that measure biomechanics, productivity, and user experience in realistic trade tasks [19], [10], [25]; in addition, velocity/acceleration sensitivity in sEMG‑only estimation [33] highlights the value of IMU fusion and multi‑condition training to preserve control accuracy across non‑periodic trade motions. Co‑design with trade workers remains essential to reconcile laboratory performance with workplace usability and to ensure attachment systems, sensor housings, and PPE interfaces meet operational constraints [9], [23].

##### 2.1.3. Control system design: data sources and neural network frameworks

Control fidelity and predictability are central to safety and acceptance. Surface EMG (sEMG) is the most mature biosignal for intent detection in wearable systems, providing a proximate measure of muscular activation. Contemporary pipelines fuse spatial convolutional feature extraction with temporal sequence models (LSTM, transformer) to stabilize noisy sEMG and enable low‑latency regression or classification for actuation [11], [12], [17]. Empirical reports show hybrid CNN-LSTM regressors achieving R^2 ≈ 0.825 (vs 0.746 for LSTM alone) and substantial MAPE reductions, demonstrating tangible performance gains from architectural fusion and careful feature engineering for joint/finger regression tasks [11]. HCSNet and similar feature‑fusion approaches achieve very high within‑subject (≈95.9%) and strong cross‑subject (≈90.4%) accuracy for lower‑limb movement prediction, indicating that combining hand‑crafted time/frequency features with learned representations improves generalizability when training data and protocols are adequate [12].

Foundational controller taxonomies emphasize impedance/admittance (force-interaction) paradigms and multi‑level architectures (task/high/low), motivating hybrid schemes that combine model‑based loops with data‑driven perception for intent estimation [27]. Systematic reviews of sensing and control highlight a key trade‑off: prior biosignals (sEMG, EEG) provide richer intent information but increase calibration burden, whereas posterior mechanical signals (IMU, encoders, force) are more robust in field settings; across ~168 surveyed lower‑limb studies this distinction recurs as a practical design constraint for fieldable controllers [28]. Deep learning reviews report high offline performance for modern architectures (gait‑phase recognition commonly 94-97.5%; motion/intention recognition often >95%), but they caution that most studies are lab-based, with dataset size requirements, inter‑subject variability and real‑time latency limits that constrain direct field translation [29].

Task‑agnostic, physiology‑driven controllers that estimate biological joint moments using temporal convolutional networks have recently shown strong generalization across diverse activities (online estimator R2 ≈ 0.79-0.86; held‑out task R2 ≈ 0.83-0.85) and measurable human outcome improvements (metabolic cost reductions reported between ~8% and ~20%), providing a promising alternative to discrete mode classifiers for unstructured construction tasks [30], [31]. These approaches also illustrate practical engineering choices, such as onboard inference hardware, device mass (4.8-7 kg), scaling/delay design (e.g., 125 ms delay), and torque clamping, that materially affect responsiveness and comfort and therefore must be considered alongside physical design trade‑offs [30], [31].

Low‑latency intent detection remains a core operational challenge. Reviews of EMG onset detection covering 156 papers document that threshold‑based methods dominate applied work (threshold methods tested 253 times across studies) and that trade‑offs between detection delay and false‑positive rates are significant (single‑threshold fastest, adaptive thresholds yield better timing at cost of more false positives in small tests) - these findings strongly inform low‑complexity, real‑time pipelines for construction exosuits where computational budgets are limited [32]. Velocity and acceleration sensitivity in sEMG‑only angle estimation further demonstrates the value of IMU fusion and multi‑condition training: sensor‑fusion schemes reduced offline RMSE from >22° (single‑condition) to ~6-8° in the best fusion configurations, with corresponding improvements in online angle‑tracking accuracy - although certain actuation choices (e.g., pneumatic muscles) can induce large latencies (~800 ms) that undermine closed‑loop responsiveness [33].

Consequently, pragmatic field designs favor compact sensor suites (sEMG + IMU) [28], conservative assistance envelopes with human‑in‑the‑loop safety guards, on‑device denoising, rapid calibration or few‑shot transfer learning, and explicit safety/watchdog layers to prevent misactuation under sensor dropout or model drift [11], [12], [14], [19], [20], [32], [33]. Research priorities include validating DL‑augmented controllers on construction populations, reducing on‑body sensor footprint, and benchmarking online closed‑loop metrics (RMSE, R2, latency, metabolic outcome) rather than offline classification accuracy alone [29], [30], [31].

Augmenting sEMG with additional modalities (EEG, IMUs) can raise classification accuracy - spectrogram‑based EEG-EMG fusion achieved ≈80.5% mean accuracy in controlled weight classification - but the marginal gains must be balanced against field practicability, increased sensor footprint, longer setup/calibration times, and vulnerability to motion artifacts [14], [20]. Consequently, pragmatic deployments favor compact sensor suites (sEMG + IMU), robust on‑device denoising and adaptive calibration, and conservative control envelopes (human‑in‑the‑loop safety guards, watchdogs) to minimize misactuation and ensure predictable assistive behaviour [11], [12], [20]. Recurrent operational issues include electrode placement sensitivity, sensor contamination (sweat, dust), displacement during dynamic movement, model drift over time, and system latencies - each requiring combined hardware, algorithmic and administrative mitigation strategies before wide field roll‑out [11], [19], [33].

Research priorities therefore include rapid few‑shot calibration or transfer‑learning for new users, sensor‑fusion strategies optimized for robustness, explicit safety layers and watchdogs, and comprehensive validation across diverse construction tasks and worker populations rather than laboratory volunteers alone [11], [12], [14], [19], [20].

##### 2.1.4. Integration Barriers, Safety, and Pathways for Exoskeleton Adoption in Construction

Synthesis of translational literature yields a pragmatic conclusion: exoskeletons have measurable biomechanical and operational benefits under controlled conditions and in short‑term pilots, but responsible scale‑up requires simultaneous attention to economic models, ergonomic refinements, social adoption dynamics and regulatory governance. Recent field trials (e.g., 2025 experimental work with 20 experienced workers) report substantial productivity and stability benefits in high‑intensity tasks (up to 59.5% productivity gain and 24.8-35.4% reduction in sway [25]), yet these trials are limited in duration, device diversity and ecological breadth, reinforcing systematic calls for longitudinal, multi‑site field evaluations [25], [26].

Economic and organizational barriers predominate in stakeholder analyses and Delphi studies: unaffordability, inaccessibility and cost justification repeatedly surface as critical adoption constraints (often >90% agreement among experts [18]), and interview studies underscore the need for demonstrable ROI, service/maintenance plans and financing models tailored to SMEs [8], [23]. Safety and risk assessments identify core hazards (snagging, interference with fall‑arrest equipment, pressure injuries, balance perturbations) and emphasize that engineering controls must be complemented by administrative measures (task selection protocols, training, fit checks and incident reporting) prior to routine deployment [5], [20], [26].

Social dynamics substantially modulate adoption. Studies of workplace culture and social influence show that managerial endorsement, peer‑led demonstrations, and incremental pilot schemes increase willingness‑to‑use [24], whereas fears of mandatory imposition, stigma or data misuse erode trust [9], [23], [16]. Ethical and governance literature therefore prescribes clear informed‑consent procedures for biosignal and health data, transparent data governance, inclusive procurement (size and PPE compatibility), and equitable financing (subsidies, rental schemes) to prevent exacerbating inequalities and to protect worker autonomy [16], [18], [6].

Policy levers advocated across reviews include targeted SME subsidy/trial programmes, standardized field‑oriented evaluation frameworks (biomechanics, productivity, ergonomics, user‑reported outcomes), procurement criteria for PPE compatibility and inclusive sizing, and mandatory adverse‑event reporting during pilots to build an evidence base for safety and effectiveness [19], [21], [23], [6]. Implementing these levers alongside device and control improvements, co‑design processes and robust longitudinal trials offer the most credible pathway to achieving scalable, safe and equitable exoskeleton adoption in construction [17], [11], [12], [16], [25].

Several strands of evidence and stakeholder analysis sharpen practical recommendations and research priorities. Industry and policy reviews emphasize that translational success depends on actionable financing and procurement pathways: subsidy or trial schemes for SMEs, rental/shared‑use models, and procurement contracts that require inclusive sizing and PPE compatibility to reduce inequitable access and avoid introducing new hazards through incompatible gear [8], [23], [18]. Mixed‑methods and workshop reports add nuance: pilot studies and iterative procurement trials in real workplaces can reveal context‑specific constraints (temperature, dust, scaffolding geometry) that laboratory tests do not capture, and they provide the operational data needed for credible ROI assessments for firms considering purchase or lease [6], [7], [9].

Ethical and social‑risk literature highlights governance issues that must be operationalized in pilots and procurement documents. Delphi and empirical studies identify a dense set of prioritized risks - unaffordability, inaccessibility, mandatory use, data misuse, and don/doff injuries - and recommend concrete mitigations such as explicit informed‑consent procedures for biosignal and health data, anonymized telemetry, and contractual protections against coercive deployment by employers [18], [16], [4]. Embedding these protections into trial design increases worker trust and the external validity of user‑reported outcomes collected during pilots [16], [24].

Operational safety remains a high‑priority area where device engineering and administrative controls must coevolve. Hazard analyses and consensus reviews recommend a layered approach: device‑level mitigations (ergonomic coupling, quick‑release interfaces, conservative actuation envelopes), site‑level administrative measures (task selection lists, pre‑use fit checks, interaction protocols with fall‑arrest systems) and monitoring/reporting systems to capture adverse events during early deployments [20], [5], [26]. Such layering aligns with occupational safety hierarchies and allows incremental rollout: begin with low‑risk, high‑benefit tasks (repetitive lifting, sack carrying) and proceed to more complex tasks only after longitudinal evidence accumulates [25], [26].

Technical research priorities align with translational needs. Control research should prioritise robustness and calibration speed (few‑shot transfer learning, on‑device denoising) over marginal improvements in laboratory accuracy, and should be validated on construction populations performing ecologically valid trade tasks [11], [12], [14], [19]. Design research must push lightweight modularity, thermal comfort, and PPE interoperability while logging objective wearability metrics (don/doff time, skin pressure maps, temperature) in field pilots [17], [4], [10]. Evaluation science requires standardized field‑oriented protocols that combine biomechanics (EMG, joint moments), productivity metrics, stability measures (IMU sway), and structured user experience instruments - a composite endpoint set recommended repeatedly in systematic reviews to improve comparability and procurement decision‑making [19], [21], [25].

#### 2.2. Phase 2: Problem Identification and Project Focus

##### Challenges in Construction and Exoskeleton Suitability

Current exoskeleton technologies do not adequately address the diverse and rapidly changing demands of construction work. Workers often require rigid support for static, high-force tasks but also need unrestricted mobility for dynamic, non-cyclical movements in chaotic environments. Most existing systems force a compromise between support and agility, and are unable to predict the highly varying movements in construction work, limiting their practical effectiveness and acceptance on site [9], [7]. as highlighted by recent reviews and stakeholder analyses that identify comfort, fit, and task suitability as persistent obstacles [8], [22]. Field studies and qualitative research further report that misalignment, bulk, and interference with PPE reduce usability and may introduce new risks, such as pressure injuries or balance perturbations [5], [20], [26].

The inability to reconcile support and mobility means exoskeletons have not fully delivered on their promise to reduce work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) in construction [4], [2]. Devices perceived as cumbersome or restrictive are frequently rejected by workers, even when they offer benefits for specific tasks [8], [23]. This slows adoption and limits the potential impact on worker health, injury-related costs, and workforce retention. Systematic reviews report WMSD prevalence rates between 59-64% among construction workers, with the lower back, shoulders, and knees most affected [2], [15], [21]. The economic burden is substantial, with annual costs exceeding \$2 billion in the US alone [1]. Without adaptable, user-friendly solutions, the full potential of exoskeletons to address these issues remains unrealized.

**Justification for the Proposed Exoskeleton Framework:** This project directly addresses the identified gap by proposing a framework for an intelligent exoskeleton. The novelty lies in its control system, which fuses two data streams-the user's biological intent via EMG signals and the exoskeleton's mechanical state via joint servo data. By feeding this combined data into a DNN, the system can learn to predict user movement and dynamically adjust support levels [10], [11]. This approach moves beyond task-specific designs to create a truly adaptive system that can provide strong static support when needed and agile assistance during movement, making it far more suitable for the dynamic reality of a construction site.

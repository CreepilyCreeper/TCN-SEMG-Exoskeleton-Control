### 1. Exoskeletons: Potential for Preventing Work-related Musculoskeletal Injuries and Disorders in Construction Workplaces
**URL:** https://blogs.cdc.gov/niosh-science-blog/2022/02/03/exoskeletons-construction/

**Summary:**
This article reviews the potential of exoskeletons to prevent work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) in construction. It summarizes recent research, discusses ergonomic interventions, and highlights both the benefits and challenges of exoskeleton adoption in construction trades.

**Key Points:**
- Construction workers have high rates of WMSDs, especially in the back, shoulders, and knees.
- Exoskeletons (passive and active) are being explored as ergonomic interventions to reduce physical strain.
- Recent studies show exoskeletons can reduce muscle activity, discomfort, and risk of injury in various construction tasks (lifting, overhead work, kneeling).
- Benefits: potential to reduce WMSDs, lightweight and easy-to-use designs, improved safety.
- Challenges: limited real-world studies, possible unintended discomfort, need for trade/task-specific designs, and potential new risks (e.g., friction, instability).
- More research is needed on effectiveness, worker acceptance, and cost-benefit analysis.

**How this reference can be used:**
- To provide background on the prevalence and cost of WMSDs in construction.
- To support the argument for exoskeletons as a promising intervention.
- To discuss both the benefits and limitations of exoskeletons in real-world construction settings.
- To cite recent studies and highlight research gaps.

**Key Statistics:**
- Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) account for approximately 65% of all injury and illness cases in construction (Choi et al., 2007).
- The economic burden of WMSDs in the US construction industry is estimated at over $2 billion annually (Liberty Mutual 2021).
- Nearly 40 million U.S. workers experience low back pain, with construction workers at higher risk than other occupations (Luckhaupt 2019).
- WMSD claims from overexertion are highest among workers aged 35–44, but claims are more severe and result in more days away from work for those aged 45–64 (Kaur 2021).
- Passive back support exoskeletons can reduce lower back muscle activities by 3–11% and reduce discomfort in various body parts (Gonsalves et al., 2021).
- Knee-assistive exoskeletons can reduce knee extension/flexion muscle activation by up to 39% and knee-ground contact forces by up to 15% (Chen et al., 2021).

**Summary:**
This systematic review and meta-analysis quantifies the prevalence of WMSDs among construction workers globally and examines demographic risk factors. The study finds a high overall prevalence, with significant variation by region, age, and gender.

**Key Points:**

- Lower back and shoulders are the most affected body regions.
- Prevalence is higher in Asia (63%) than America (39%) or Africa (52%).

- Experience may reduce risk; ergonomic interventions are recommended.
- Limitations include high heterogeneity, reliance on self-reported data, and underrepresentation of some regions and female workers.

Multiple CNN architectures and fusion methods were tested to classify task weight, with time–frequency (spectrogram) models showing the highest accuracy. **Key Points:**

- Time–frequency domain (spectrogram) models outperform time-domain models.
- The best fusion method achieved 80.51% mean accuracy for three-class task weight classification.
- Model performance is affected by movement speed and task weight; slower movements yield higher accuracy.
- The study provides a foundation for future development of robust, user-adaptive exoskeleton control systems.

**How this reference can be used:**

- To provide evidence of improved classification accuracy using CNN-based fusion.

**Key Statistics:**
